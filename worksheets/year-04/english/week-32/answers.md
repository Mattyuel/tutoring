### Part F — marking notes
**Model (Option 1, ~130 words):**
*Wattleseed Pikelets* (makes about 12)
*You will need:* 1 cup (150 g) self-raising flour, 1 tablespoon caster sugar, 1 teaspoon ground roasted wattleseed (store-bought), 1 egg, ¾ cup (185 mL) milk, a little butter, 2 bowls, a whisk, a frying pan, an egg flip.
1. *First, whisk the egg and milk together in a small bowl.*
2. *Mix the flour, sugar and wattleseed in a large bowl.*
3. *Pour in the egg mixture and stir gently until smooth.*
4. *Ask an adult to heat a frying pan over medium heat, then grease it lightly with butter.*
5. *Carefully drop 1 tablespoon of batter into the pan for each pikelet.*
6. *Cook until bubbles appear on top (about 1 minute).*
7. *Flip each pikelet quickly and cook for another 30–60 seconds.*
*Safety: Only an adult should use the stove, because the pan gets very hot.*

**Model (Option 2, ~110 words):** *How to Measure Ingredients Accurately. You will need: measuring cups, measuring spoons, a jug, a kitchen scale, a knife. 1. First, spoon the flour lightly into the cup; don't press it down. 2. Level the top carefully with the flat edge of a knife. 3. For liquids, place the jug on a flat bench. 4. Pour slowly until the liquid reaches the line, then check it with your eyes level with the mark. 5. When you use a spoon, fill it and level it, so that you don't use a heaped spoonful. 6. Before you weigh food, set the scale to zero with the empty bowl on it.*

A strong response: goal; complete list with amounts; numbered, correctly ordered steps; imperative verbs; ≥3 units; sequence words; precise adverbs; a fronted adverbial with comma; a safety note with a reason. Watch for: steps written as a recount (*I mixed…*); vague amounts; numbering but merging several actions into one long step.

## Teacher notes
- **Text set this week:** Text 1 information (inform) · Text 2 recipe (instruct) · Text 3 poem (entertain) · Text 4 persuasive letter (persuade); F3 adds a short information text on spoon sizes. Q19 is the key "compare the texts" task; do it orally first if time is short.
- **Common misconceptions:** "natural means safe"; "processed means unhealthy"; "all wattles are the same"; that Aboriginal seed processing is only in the past (¶4 stresses continuity); writing procedures as recounts (*I mixed…*); thinking all tablespoons are the same size.
- **Safety:** the recipe and homework say *make with an adult* and *store-bought wattleseed only*. Reinforce: never eat wild seeds or plants without expert knowledge. Check for allergies (gluten, dairy, egg) before any cooking at home.
- **Cultural respect:** Text 1 keeps claims general ("in many parts of Australia"); practices differed between language groups and Country. Texts 3 and 4 do not portray specific cultural stories. If the student wants to know more, suggest local Noongar resources (e.g. a Noongar-run cultural centre or the WA Museum) rather than generalising further.
- **Recipe check:** 2 cups SR flour = 300 g (Australian cup ≈ 150 g flour); 300 g flour with about 225 mL liquid (185 mL milk + 40 mL soaking water) gives a soft scone-like dough, in line with standard damper ratios (e.g. 3 cups flour to about 1½ cups liquid). A 15 cm damper at 200 °C for 25–30 min is typical. Pikelet facts (1 cup SR flour, 1 egg, ¾ cup milk, 1 tbsp batter each, bubbles after about 1 min) follow standard Australian pikelet recipes. Soaking ground wattleseed in hot water first is common practice and softens its grittiness.
- **If the student finishes early:** F7 and F8 are good real-world challenges; F4 is a fun way to check the science.
- **If the student struggles:** act out a procedure (the tutor follows instructions *literally*: "put the flour in" → tip the whole bag) to show why precision matters.
- **Sources / fact-check:**
  - *Acacia* is Australia's largest genus of flowering plants, with around 1,000 Australian species (of roughly 1,300+ worldwide), so "hundreds of kinds, more than any other country" is safely hedged. Golden wattle (*Acacia pycnantha*) is Australia's national floral emblem (proclaimed 1988). Wattles are legumes (Fabaceae) and bear seeds in pods. — Australian National Botanic Gardens / Australian Plant Information; Australian Government (national symbols).
  - Wattle seeds have hard coats, form long-lived soil seed banks, and many germinate after fire heat breaks the coat (physical dormancy); hot-water soaking is the standard propagation method. — ANBG / Australian Native Plants Society propagation notes. Planting depth "about 5 mm" and "often within a few weeks" are deliberately approximate.
  - Only some species have edible seeds (e.g. *Acacia victoriae*, elegant wattle, the main commercial species; others used traditionally include mulga and other inland species — not named to students); some species' seeds contain compounds that can be harmful. Hence the strong "never eat wild seeds" message. — CSIRO / RIRDC (AgriFutures) reports on edible wattle seed.
  - Aboriginal peoples parched/roasted seeds in hot sand or coals, ground them on grindstones (lower grinding slab and upper hand stone) and cooked seed dough in ashes; grindstones are widespread in the archaeological record across Australia, with seed-grinding dating back many thousands of years. Kept as "thousands of years" (some sites suggest far older dates, but these are debated). — Australian Museum; National Museum of Australia; published archaeology (e.g. Cuddie Springs). Gender roles and specific groups deliberately not stated.
  - Colonisation: land clearing and grazing by sheep and cattle disrupted access to Country and traditional foods; many Aboriginal people were forcibly removed from their land. — standard Year 4 HASS content.
  - Today: roasted ground wattleseed is sold as a bush-food flavouring; flavour commonly described as coffee-, chocolate- or hazelnut-like; some Aboriginal communities and enterprises are involved in harvesting and selling bush foods. Phrased with "some". — AgriFutures Australia native foods; bush-food industry descriptions.
  - Roasting darkens the seeds, develops a roasted aroma and makes them brittle for grinding; ground wattleseed is slightly gritty (hence soaking). Roasting/baking are irreversible changes; melting and freezing are reversible.
  - F3 / Connections: Australian metric tablespoon = 20 mL (4 tsp); US/UK tablespoon ≈ 15 mL; Australian metric cup = 250 mL; teaspoon = 5 mL. — Standards Australia measuring utensils; Food Standards. All arithmetic checked: 65 mL, 75 g, 375 g, scaled table, 12:25 pm, 45 mL = 2¼ tbsp, F7 = 5 dampers, 1.5 kg, 150 g, 10 tsp = 2½ tbsp, 10:45 am.
  - Text 4: many native plants are suited to Perth's dry summers and need less water once established (hedged with "many" and "usually"); wattles and grevilleas attract birds and insects. Ari, the principal and the café are fictional.
  - Text 3 is original; its science matches Text 1 ¶1 (flowering late winter–spring for many species; pods dry and split; fire heat then rain → germination).
- **Next week builds on:** *Saving the Western Swamp Tortoise*: objective vs emotive language. Text 4 (emotive words, *usually* as an honest qualifier) and the information-vs-poem comparison (F9, Q19) lead straight into telling objective from emotive writing and identifying text purpose.
