### Part F — marking notes
**Model (Option 1, ~120 words):**
*A compass is a simple tool that helps people find their way. Inside it, a thin magnetic needle can spin freely. Deep inside, Earth behaves like a giant magnet, so when you hold the compass flat and still, the needle slowly turns until one end points roughly north. Because the needle always settles in the same direction, you can easily work out north, south, east and west. For hundreds of years, sailors have relied on compasses, especially on cloudy nights when they could not see the stars. Today, bushwalkers still carry them because they need no batteries. As a result, a compass can guide you safely home even when other equipment stops working.*

**Model (Option 2, ~110 words):** *In recycling plants, steel and aluminium cans arrive completely mixed together. Before they can be recycled, they must be separated. First, the cans travel steadily along a conveyor belt past a strong magnet. Because steel is made mostly of iron, the magnet attracts the steel cans and pulls them quickly out of the stream. Meanwhile, the aluminium cans keep moving along the belt, because aluminium is not magnetic. Finally, each metal drops into its own bin. As a result, both metals can be melted down separately and made into new products. Without magnets, workers would have to sort millions of cans slowly by hand.*

A strong response: opening statement; logical how/why sequence with cause-and-effect connectives; accurate science vocabulary; ≥4 underlined adverbials (incl. a fronted one with a comma); a precise adverb (*steadily, freely, safely*); a complex sentence; a concluding sentence about why it matters. Watch for: commas missing after fronted adverbials; the misconception that magnets attract all metal; saying a compass points "to the North Pole exactly".

28. e.g. *First, he holds the north end of magnet 1 near one end of magnet 2. If they repel, that end of magnet 2 is also north, because like poles repel. **F8.** Hold the **end** of bar 1 against the **middle** of bar 2. A magnet's force is weakest in the middle and strongest at the poles. If bar 1 is the magnet, its pole will attract the middle of the plain steel bar strongly. If bar 1 is the plain steel, it will hardly be pulled by the middle of the magnet. Swap and compare: the bar whose **end** pulls strongly on the other's **middle** is the magnet.
**F9.** For: e.g. less friction means fast, smooth, quiet travel; could link the airport and city quickly; electric so no exhaust fumes from the train itself. ## Teacher notes
- **Common misconceptions:** "magnets attract all metals"; "bigger magnets are always stronger" (Mia's data deliberately invites discussion — shape and material matter); "the compass points to the North Pole exactly"; "cardboard/paper blocks magnetism" (it is mostly distance); "magnetism and gravity are the same thing" (both are non-contact forces, but different).
- **Pole puzzle subtlety (Earth):** the end of a compass needle that points north is called its *north-seeking* or north pole. Because unlike poles attract, Earth's magnetic pole in the Arctic actually behaves like a magnet's *south* pole. That is why the giant-magnet bar in the diagram is deliberately **unlabelled**. Only raise this with a student who asks.
- **Diagram for Q6:** the field-line arrows point northward along the surface (the horizontal direction a compass needle aligns with). The diagram is simplified (real field lines also dip into the ground at an angle).
- **Safety:** small strong (neodymium) magnets are dangerous if swallowed; keep magnets away from bank cards and electronics. Homework tells students not to test electronics.
- **If the student finishes early:** F4 can become a real investigation; F8 and Part G Q28 are excellent "think aloud" puzzles.
- **If the student struggles:** act out adverbials — say a verb (*walk*) and have them add *how* (*slowly*), *where* (*to the door*), *when* (*after lunch*). Use real magnets for Q4.
- **Sources / fact-check:**
  - Magnetic force is a non-contact force acting through non-magnetic materials (paper, plastic, water); attracted materials iron, nickel, cobalt and most steels; not aluminium, copper, wood, plastic, glass. *Note:* some stainless steels (austenitic, e.g. many sinks/cutlery) are not magnetic, hence "most kinds of steel". — standard school physics (e.g. Australian Curriculum Year 4 Physical sciences; Science Learning Hub NZ "Magnets").
  - Like poles repel, unlike poles attract; force strongest at the poles; iron filings show field pattern. — standard.
  - Earth's magnetic field (generated in the molten outer core) makes Earth behave like a giant bar magnet; compass needle aligns with it and points roughly north (magnetic north ≠ true north; declination in Perth is small, about 0–2°). Phrased as "roughly north" and "when you hold a compass flat and still" because compasses are unreliable near the magnetic poles. — Geoscience Australia (geomagnetism); British Geological Survey.
  - Compasses used for navigation "for hundreds of years"; F3: Chinese use for ship navigation about a thousand years ago (Song dynasty texts, early 1100s; Shen Kuo described a magnetic needle in 1088). Phrased with "Historians think". Lodestone = naturally magnetised magnetite.
  - Fridge door seals contain a flexible magnetic strip (most modern fridges); loudspeakers/headphones use permanent magnets with a coil; scrapyard lifting electromagnets are switched off to release loads; recycling facilities use magnets (overband or drum/roller magnets) to remove steel; aluminium is separated in other ways (e.g. eddy-current separators — not mentioned to students). Most Australian drink cans are aluminium; food tins are mostly tin-plated steel.
  - Maglev: magnetic levitation lifts the train off the guideway and linear motors propel it — no wheel–rail friction. Shanghai Maglev (Transrapid technology) links Pudong International Airport with Longyang Road station; inaugurated 2002, regular commercial service from 2004 — phrased as "since the early 2000s". Described only as "one of the fastest passenger trains in the world"; no speeds given. ("Between Pudong Airport and the city" — strictly it ends on the edge of the city centre at Longyang Road; accurate enough at Year 4.)
  - Animal magnetoreception: evidence for loggerhead and other sea turtles (e.g. Lohmann lab research) and migratory birds (e.g. European robins); mechanisms still under research — hedged with "scientists think", "may", "still working out". Green and loggerhead turtles nest along the Ningaloo coast (WA DBCA / Ningaloo Turtle Program).
  - Homework: binnacle = the stand/housing for a ship's compass, near the helm; liquid-filled compasses damp needle movement; iron/steel deflects compasses (ships' compasses are corrected for this).
  - Connections data (Mia's magnets) are invented sample data for practice, not real measurements; all arithmetic checked (difference 10, ratio 3, total 32; 9/12 = 3/4, 1/4 × 120 = 30; grid route B1 → B4 → F4).
