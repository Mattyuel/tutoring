## Teacher notes
- **Common misconceptions:**
  - Mixing up *factor* and *multiple* (rhyme: "**F**actors **F**it into, **M**ultiples **M**ake more").
  - Missing factor pairs — insist on working up systematically from 1 until the pairs repeat; forgetting 1 × *n* and *n* itself.
  - Thinking 1 is prime (it has only one factor), or that 2 is not prime because it's even.
  - Applying the "digit sum" trick to 2, 5 or 10 (those depend on the last digit only), or to 4 and 6 (not taught yet).
  - Decimal patterns: writing 2.10 after 2.9 or 1.100 after 1.95 — the "whole-number thinking" error from Week 14. Have them say "two and nine tenths, three wholes" aloud or use a number line.
  - Fraction patterns: writing 4/4 or 1 4⁄4 instead of 1; not converting improper fractions to mixed numbers.
  - "Odd numbers are prime" — 9, 15, 21, 27, 51, 57 and 91 are good counter-examples.
- **If the student finishes early:** F12 (lockers) and F9 make good open investigations — extend F12 to 100 lockers, or ask "which number from 1 to 100 has the most factors?" (60, 72, 84, 90 and 96 tie with 12 each). For primes, extend the sieve to 100 (25 primes).
- **If the student struggles:** build arrays with counters or square tiles for factor pairs; use a hundred chart and coloured transparent counters for multiples; use a ruler/measuring jug marked in quarters for fraction patterns, and money (5c, 10c, 25c steps) for decimal patterns.
- **Sources / fact-check:** The Sieve of Eratosthenes is named after Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–194 BCE), so "about 2 200 years ago" is a safe round figure. Goldbach's conjecture (1742) is still unproven. An Australian metric cup is 250 mL. The bus timetable, savings amounts, prices and flashing lights are invented for the problems.
- **Next week builds on (Week 32 — unknowns and pre-algebra):** factor pairs → missing-factor number sentences (□ × 6 = 48); pattern rules → input/output tables and inverse operations; Connections next week uses perimeter with a missing side written as □.
