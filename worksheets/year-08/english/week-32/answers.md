### Part F
22. The media sit *in the middle* between events, or people with power, and citizens, carrying information across that gap. That is why their choices matter so much. *Bias* as "a slant" fits framing because a framed story is not false; it is **tilted**, so the reader's view slides towards one conclusion, like a ball on a sloping table (or a weighted bowl in lawn bowls, which curves).

### Part G: comparative media analysis, marking notes
**Model response (≈285 words)**

> Although Report 1 and Report 3 describe the same council vote, and neither contains an obvious falsehood, they frame the North Beach decision in opposite ways. Report 1 presents a community wronged by its council, whereas Report 3 presents a council delivering an improvement.
>
> The difference is clear before the first paragraph ends. Report 1's headline, "Beach parking axed despite community outcry", uses the violent verb "axed" and the emotive "outcry", and its photo of queuing cars makes the reader see demand that is being ignored. In contrast, Report 3's "Shire unveils $1.8m North Beach upgrade" sounds like a ribbon-cutting, and its smiling photo of the Shire President comes straight from public relations. Each lead then foregrounds what suits its frame: the 5–4 vote and "almost 60 per cent" opposition in Report 1, and the future benefits that beachgoers "will enjoy" in Report 3.
>
> Voices and omissions deepen the contrast. Three of Report 1's four sources oppose the plan, and the Shire's reason, erosion, is buried in the final sentence, where an editor would cut first. Report 3 quotes only the Shire, relies on a "200 per cent" rise in falls from a base of one, and hides a majority against the plan behind the passive "the decision was made following extensive community consultation". Both reports omit Sam Okafor, whose mixed view fits neither frame.
>
> Report 1 is the fairer of the two, because its figures are accurate and it at least mentions the Shire's reason, while Report 3 reads like a media release. However, neither gives the whole picture. A Tuart Bay voter who read only one would make a decision based on half the story, which is why a democracy needs many outlets and readers who compare them.

## Fast-finisher bank: answers and marking notes
**F1.** e.g. Neutral: *Tuart Bay council votes 5–4 to move North Beach parking*. Surf club: *Rescue access kept as North Beach car park moves inland*. Accessibility: *ACROD bays stay at beach as main car park moves*.

**F2.** e.g. *The Shire of Tuart Bay has voted 5–4 to replace North Beach's 120-bay car park with a 90-bay car park 350 m inland, after 240 of 412 public submissions opposed the plan.* (34 words.)

**F3.** Supports closing: *The car park is only about 30% full on an average weekday.* Opposes: *The car park is full by 9 am on summer weekends.* Fair: *The car park is about 30% full on an average weekday but full by about 9 am on summer weekends and public holidays, according to Shire counts.*

**F4.** Look for: a lead built on accessibility (ACROD bays and drop-off zone kept at the beach entrance); Sam Okafor quoted early, including his request for shade; the boardwalk distance and the 5–4 vote lower down. Facts that move up: the ACROD bays, drop-off zone, boardwalk length and the 5-minute walk; Joan Whitby's concern becomes a natural counter-voice.

**F5.** e.g. *I am younger than the dune, / but I know its moods. / Every summer morning the cars came to me first, / sandy feet, coffee orders shouted over the wind. / Now they will park on Dune Road / and walk past me with their eyes on the sea.* The reader's sympathy shifts to the human cost; the same techniques (first person, personification) create a different frame. Reward awareness that the *form* is not neutral.

**F6.** Strong answers: feeds choose and order stories (selection and placement) for each user, often by what similar users clicked, so a person may repeatedly see one kind of frame on many issues; this *may* narrow what they see (a "filter bubble"), but researchers disagree about how strong the effect is, and many people still meet a range of sources. Look for hedges (*may*, *can*, *some research suggests*).

**F7.** The Constitution sets up representative government with members "directly chosen by the people" (ss 7 and 24). The High Court reasoned that real choice needs free political discussion, so the freedom follows *by implication*. It limits laws; it is not a personal right; it covers political communication, not all speech. For a written guarantee: clarity, and protection of non-political speech. Against: entrenching rights shifts power to judges; existing protections and democratic culture work; a guarantee is very hard to change because it needs a referendum. Reward a concession and a rebuttal, and accurate use of *implied*.

**F8.** Look for an analytical register (*The vote reflects…*, *The dispute illustrates…*), evidence from both sides (58% of submissions opposed; 12 m of retreat; summer-weekend demand vs 30% weekday use; $1.2m state grant), an explanation of the wider pattern (old coastal infrastructure built where shorelines are now retreating), and no first-person argument or loaded language.

## Teacher notes
- **Program link:** builds directly on Week 31 (news structure, objectivity, Reports A and B on fictional Cyclone Harlow). Week 31 taught obvious, sensational bias; Week 32 teaches subtle bias in reports that all *look* objective, and adds opinion and creative texts to contrast persuasion and perspective with framing. If time allows, briefly compare Report B (Week 31) with Text 4 (both emotive, but one claims to be news).
- **Week 33** (*The Mongol Empire*: reputation and historiography, evaluating claims) builds on this week's ideas. A historical "reputation" is a frame built by whoever wrote the sources: many accounts of the Mongols were written by people they conquered, while other sources emphasise trade along the Silk Road and religious tolerance. Students should carry over selection and omission, *whose voices survive*, loaded language and checking claims against evidence. The *hero or villain?* argument is a frame choice.
- **Common misconceptions:** "biased = lying" (all three reports are accurate sentence by sentence); "facts and numbers = objective" (Report 3); "the report I agree with is unbiased" (Report 2, Q23); "all media is biased, so nothing can be trusted" (challenge this with ¶7: fairness is a matter of degree); a percentage increase always means a large change.
- **Running it:** Part D is the heart of the lesson; if short of time, do Q11, Q12, Q13 and Q16 and set Q15 and Q17 for homework. Part E Q21 can be done orally. Read the poem aloud.
- **If the student struggles:** have them highlight every fact in the fact file that appears in each report (one pass per report), then look at what is left unhighlighted.
- **Sensitivity:** keep discussion of real outlets general. If the student brings up a current real story in the homework, focus on technique, not on which side is right.

### Sources and fact-check notes
Only facts in Text 1 and the homework's cockatoo reference are real; claims are kept general and hedged.
- **Compulsory voting:** compulsory voting for federal elections was legislated in 1924 (first used in the 1925 election), for enrolled citizens aged 18 and over (Australian Electoral Commission). **WA local government elections are voluntary** (WA Electoral Commission). Students used these in Week 16.
- **Fitzgerald Inquiry (Qld, 1987–1989):** prompted by Phil Dickie's reporting in the *Courier-Mail* and the ABC *Four Corners* program "The Moonlight State" (May 1987). It led to the conviction and jailing of former Police Commissioner Terry Lewis and to convictions of several former state ministers, plus major reforms (e.g. the Criminal Justice Commission). Sources: Queensland Government / Crime and Corruption Commission history; ABC archives.
- **No express free-speech guarantee; implied freedom of political communication:** recognised by the High Court in 1992 in *Australian Capital Television Pty Ltd v Commonwealth* and *Nationwide News Pty Ltd v Wills*; confirmed and restated in *Lange v Australian Broadcasting Corporation* (1997), which established that it is a limit on legislative power, not a personal right, and that laws burdening it can be valid if reasonably appropriate and adapted to a legitimate end (refined in later cases such as *McCloy v NSW*, 2015). Based on ss 7 and 24 of the Constitution ("directly chosen by the people"). Sources: High Court judgments; Australian Human Rights Commission; Parliamentary Education Office. The text simplifies the test as "a reasonable way of achieving a legitimate purpose".
- **US First Amendment** protects freedom of speech and of the press (US Constitution, 1791).
- **Media concentration:** deliberately hedged ("researchers have often described… as highly concentrated"); no rankings or percentages given, since figures vary by study and year. Closures of many regional and local newspapers (especially around 2020) are widely documented, e.g. by the Public Interest Journalism Initiative's tracking of news contractions; this is stated only in general terms.
- **ABC and SBS:** governed by the *Australian Broadcasting Corporation Act 1983* (the board's duty to maintain the ABC's independence and integrity) and the *Special Broadcasting Service Act 1991*. The ABC is funded mainly by government; SBS also carries advertising, which is why the text says "largely".
- **Australian Press Council:** established 1976; handles complaints about member print and online publications against its Standards of Practice. It cannot fine publications (hence Q2e is NS from the text; in fact, it can require publication of adjudications). Broadcast complaints go to ACMA (not in the student text).
- **Framing:** definition paraphrased from R. Entman (1993), *Framing: toward clarification of a fractured paradigm* ("select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient"); common news frames (conflict, human interest, economic consequences, responsibility, morality) from Semetko & Valkenburg (2000). "Fourth estate" isn't used in the text (its origin is disputed; Carlyle attributed it to Burke).
- **Dune science (Connections, poem):** foredunes act as sand reserves; storm waves erode the beach and dune (forming a scarp) and move sand offshore to bars; calmer conditions return sand to the beach, and onshore winds carry it into the dune, where vegetation such as spinifex traps and binds it. Hard surfaces and loss of vegetation stop this recovery. In south-west WA, winter storms typically erode beaches and summer conditions rebuild them, which the poem simplifies. WA's *State Planning Policy 2.6: State Coastal Planning Policy* includes avoiding hazards, managed or planned retreat, accommodation and protection in its risk-management approach, which is why moving a car park back is a realistic council option. A 2019 study commissioned by the WA Government identified dozens of coastal erosion "hotspots" (not used in the student text).
- **Carnaby's black cockatoo** (homework): listed as **Endangered** under the *EPBC Act* and WA legislation; it breeds and feeds in the Wheatbelt and south-west. The wind farm, town, company and all people are fictional.
- **Etymology:** *media* < Latin *medium*; *journal* < Old French *jornal* < Latin *diurnalis*; *bias* < French *biais* (slant; the lawn-bowls sense dates from the 16th century); *propaganda* < *Congregatio de Propaganda Fide*, founded by Pope Gregory XV in 1622; *objective/subjective* < Latin *iacere*; *editor* < Latin *edere*.
- **Maths checked:** 240/412 = 58.3%, 152/412 = 36.9%, 20/412 = 4.9%; 240/152 = 1.58; truncated bars 100 : 12 ≈ 8.3; 12 m ÷ 15 years = 0.8 m/year; 350 ÷ 0.8 = 437.5; falls 1 → 3 = +200%; bays 120 → 94 (90 + 4 ACROD) = 21.7% fewer (Report 1's "90 compared with 120" implies 25%); State share 1.2/1.8 = 66.7%; Shire share \$600 000 ÷ 2400 properties = \$250 per property.
- **Everything about Tuart Bay, North Beach, the Shire of Tuart Bay, the Coastline Courier, EarthWire WA, South West Daily, Mulgara Flats, Westwind Energy, the Wheatbelt Voice and the Westerly Wire is fictional**, including all people quoted.
