## 1 · Read

> ### The Right Cell for the Job
>
> **¶1** Although every cell in your body began from a single fertilised egg, your cells are not all the same. As an embryo develops, cells become **specialised**: their shape and contents change so that they can carry out one particular job very well. Scientists call this process *differentiation*.
>
> **¶2** Red blood cells are a striking example. They are shaped like discs that are pressed in on both sides, which gives them a large surface area for absorbing oxygen. Mature red blood cells in humans have no nucleus, so there is more room for haemoglobin, the iron-containing protein that carries oxygen. Because they lack a nucleus, however, they cannot repair themselves, and each one survives only about four months.
>
> **¶3** Nerve cells, or neurons, carry electrical signals around the body. Some have extremely long, thin extensions; the nerve cells that run from the base of the spine to the toes can be around a metre long. Their many branching ends allow a single neuron to connect with thousands of others.
>
> **¶4** Plants have specialised cells too. Root hair cells have long, finger-like extensions that push between soil particles, increasing the area through which water can be absorbed. On the underside of leaves, pairs of curved **guard cells** open and close tiny pores called *stomata*, controlling how much water vapour escapes and how much carbon dioxide enters.
>
> **¶5** In every case, the structure of the cell matches its function. Biologists use this principle constantly: when they discover a cell with an unusual shape, their first question is usually, "What job does that shape make possible?"
