### Part E
**14.** a) Arrangement 1: one tilted square, area **$c^2$**. Arrangement 2: two squares, total area **$a^2 + b^2$**.
b) Both big squares have the same area $(a + b)^2$, and both contain the same four triangles. ## Teacher notes
- **Common misconceptions:**
  - Adding the squares when finding a shorter side (Q6). Habit to build: *"Is my answer shorter than the hypotenuse?"*
  - $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = a + b$ (e.g. $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 7$). Counter-example: 3–4–5.
  - Squaring decimals: $1.5^2 = 2.25$, not 2.5 or 3.
  - Rounding the base diagonal before finding a space diagonal (Q15) — keep $\sqrt{160}$ or 160 exactly.
  - Using the converse on the wrong side: $c$ must be the **longest** side.
  - Thinking "looks right" counts (triangle E in Q5).
- **If the student finishes early:** Fast-finisher F9–F12; ask them to prove the converse (construct a right-angled triangle with legs $a$, $b$; its hypotenuse must be $c$; then SSS congruence, Week 14). Euclid's formula (Q16) generates *all* primitive triples when $m, n$ are coprime and not both odd — a good research prompt.
- **If the student struggles:** use squared paper — draw the 3–4–5 triangle with squares on the sides and count unit squares; restrict to Pythagorean triples before surds; label $c$ first every time.
- **Calculator use:** Parts A–C are non-calculator except items marked 🖩; Part D is calculator. Exact (surd) answers are expected whenever no rounding is requested.
- **Facts and contexts:**
  - Netball court 30.5 m × 15.25 m: the standard court dimensions in the World Netball rules.
  - The "1 in 14" ramp rule is presented as hypothetical ("suppose"). For reference, 1:14 is the commonly cited maximum gradient for an accessible ramp under Australian Standard AS 1428.1 — check the current standard before quoting it as fact.
  - The ladder "1 out for every 4 up" (≈75°) rule of thumb appears widely in Australian workplace-safety guidance; it is phrased as a rule of thumb, not a regulation.
  - Ladder, builder, yacht, tent, kite and gate measurements are invented for the exercise.
  - The 3–4–5 "rope-stretcher" story (homework Q8) is kept deliberately vague ("builders and surveyors have long used…"), because claims about ancient Egyptian use are not well documented.
- **Next week (Week 32) builds on:** distance between points on the Cartesian plane (Q10, X4, F10) → the distance formula $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$ and midpoints; 3D diagonals (Q15, F11) → space diagonals in boxes and prisms; more applications (bearings-style navigation, Q13).
