## Teacher notes
- **Why this topic first:** day and night is concrete, can be demonstrated with an orange and a torch, and fixes a very common confusion (*"the Sun goes down"*). It also separates two ideas children often mix up: **spin = day**, **orbit = year**.
- **Misconceptions to listen for:** the Sun moves around the Earth; the Sun "goes to sleep" or hides behind hills; clouds or the Moon make night; it is night everywhere at the same time; the Earth is flat.
- **Language note:** we say "the Sun rises" for convenience — tell her this is how it *looks* from Earth, not what is really happening.
- **If she struggles:** act it out — she is the Earth, a lamp is the Sun; she turns slowly on the spot and says "sunrise… day… sunset… night".
- **If she flies through it:** Keep going K3 and K6 (sunrise/sunset positions), and ask *"Why don't we feel the Earth spinning?"*
- **Accuracy notes:** Earth rotates once about every 24 hours (≈ 23 h 56 min relative to the stars; 24 h solar day); orbit ≈ 365¼ days; the Sun is a star made mostly of hot, glowing gas (plasma — simplified to "gas"); when it is midday in Perth it is around 4–5 am in England depending on the season, so "children in England are fast asleep" is accurate. Tawny frogmouths are nocturnal Australian birds that camouflage as branches by day; many bats use echolocation to catch insects.
