# Year 3 English — Week 32 · Answers & Teacher Notes

> **Track:** Year 3 English (comprehension & world knowledge), pitched at a typical-to-strong Year 3 level with challenge. Currently used with a Year 4 student who is behind in comprehension. Core ≈ 70 min; Keep going ≈ 25 min.

### Part H · Writing — model answer (≈115 words)
*Today Nan took Ruby and me to Cottesloe Beach. It was scorching, so the sand burned my feet. Nan gave us each a block of ice for a race. First, I put mine on a metal tray in the sun, because I thought metal was cold. Ruby wrapped hers in a tea towel and put it in the esky. Ten minutes later, my ice was a puddle! Nan explained that heat moves from hot things to cold things. The sun heated my tray, so heat rushed into my ice. As a result, it melted fast. Ruby's insulators slowed the heat down, so she won. Next time I will use two tea towels!*

**Marking notes:** orientation (when, where, who) · events in order with time words · a correct **why** (heat moves from hot to cold; conductor vs insulator) · ≥2 *because / so / as a result* · past tense throughout · sentence punctuation. Pick **one** spelling (ideally an *-ed* word) to correct together rather than marking every error.

## Teacher notes
- **Why this topic and this format:** heat is everyday and testable at the table, and the story lets her **see** the science happen (burning feet, a melting ice block) before it is stated plainly in Text 2. Three text types on one topic let her notice that **purpose changes the writing** (story = past tense and dialogue; information = present tense and headings; advert = "you", exclamations and claims).
- **Misconceptions to listen for:**
  - **"Cold moves into things"** / "the cold leaks out". Correct it every time: *heat* moves, from hot to cold; cold just means "less heat".
  - **"A jumper/blanket makes heat"** or "a tea towel will warm the ice". Your body makes the heat; insulators slow heat moving either way — so a tea towel keeps ice **colder** for longer.
  - **"Metal is colder than wood"** at the same room temperature. They are the same temperature; metal conducts heat away from your hand faster, so it **feels** colder. (On a hot day, metal in the sun feels hotter for the same reason.)
  - "Insulators stop heat" / "an esky keeps ice frozen forever". They only slow it.
- **If she struggles:** do the touch test — metal tap or spoon vs a wooden table in the same room; then hold an ice cube in a bare hand vs through a folded tea towel. Reduce Keep going and spend more time on Chunks 3–4 and Q24.
- **If she flies through it:** K4 f, K6, K7; or ask *"Why does a metal slide in the sun burn your legs but a plastic one doesn't burn as much?"* and *"Why do saucepans have plastic handles?"*
- **Fact-check / sources** (all simplified, not dumbed down):
  - Heat flows spontaneously from hotter to colder bodies until thermal equilibrium (second law of thermodynamics; standard Year 3 Physical sciences content: "heat can be produced in many ways and can move from one object to another", SCSA / Australian Curriculum Science Year 3).
  - Perceived coldness of metal vs wood at the same temperature is due to higher thermal conductivity (and effusivity) of metal — heat leaves the skin faster.
  - Sand vs sea: water's specific heat (~4.2 J/g·°C) is about five times that of dry sand (~0.8 J/g·°C), and the sea mixes, so dry beach sand gets much hotter in the sun. "35 °C air" is a typical hot Perth summer day (Perth's average January maximum is about 31 °C, BoM); Perth/Cottesloe sea temperature in early summer is about 20–22 °C.
  - Body core temperature about 37 °C. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C (at sea level).
  - Foam (polystyrene / polyurethane) insulates because it is mostly trapped air; trapped air, wool, wood and plastic are poor conductors; most metals (silver, copper, aluminium) are good conductors — silver is the best metal conductor of heat (for Q26 g).
  - Hot chocolate data (K4) is modelled with Newton's law of cooling (room 22 °C, start 80 °C, k ≈ 0.046/min), values rounded: 80, 68, 59, 51, 45, 40, 36 — checked in Python.
  - Homework animal facts: fur/feathers trap insulating air; birds fluff feathers when cold; whales and seals have blubber; dogs pant; kangaroos lick their forearms for evaporative cooling; koalas hug cooler tree trunks on hot days (Briscoe et al., 2014, *Biology Letters*); bobtails (shingleback lizards, common in Perth gardens) bask to warm up because they cannot make enough body heat.
  - The Chill-Max Esky is a **made-up** product; "Esky" itself is an Australian brand name now used for any cool box.
- **What to record** in her notes file (`students/<name>.md`):
  - Her Q4 prediction (did she say "cold goes into my hand"?) and whether it changed by Q27; her Part A e) answer about jumpers.
  - Whether she could find cause and effect from *because / so / as a result* (Checks 1–3, Q8 chain).
  - Story elements (Q9): could she state the **problem** and **resolution** without help?
  - Fact vs opinion (Q22) and spotting the false claim (Q21) — first persuasive text on this track.
  - Accuracy on 👆 vs 🔍 vs 💭, and on "not in the text" (Q26 g–h) — compare with Week 31.
  - Spelling errors (Part A Q3, Q33, Q34, Q35) and whether past tense stays consistent in her writing (Part H).
